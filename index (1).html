The Problem

A Park That Everybody
Walks Through

Montefiore Park sits at Tremont and Morris Avenues in the Bronx. Despite extraordinary murals and cultural significance, its design actively discourages lingering. Three core failures define the existing condition.

Problem 01

A Corridor, Not a Place

Montefiore Park's spatial layout offers no reason to deviate from the straight-line path between entrances. No seating nodes, no spatial variety, no visual interruptions. Every design signal says keep walking.

The result: under two minutes of average dwell time — despite six commissioned mural panels that took artists months to create.

Problem 02

Seating Faces the Wrong Direction

Every existing bench is oriented parallel to the pedestrian path. A seated visitor's natural sightline faces other pedestrians walking by — not the murals lining the walls.

Seating orientation is the most direct physical cue telling visitors what they are supposed to look at. The benches need to face the art.

Problem 03

No Light, No Programming, No Life After Dark

After 6pm, Montefiore Park empties. One weak streetlight leaves the mural walls in shadow, making the space feel unsafe and erasing any chance of evening community use.

Without events, markets, or performances, there is no recurring reason to visit the park as a destination. Culture without an audience fades.

Cultural Context

The Existing Murals
Have Authors

The proposal treats the site’s mural program as existing cultural infrastructure, not decoration. Public documentation identifies the Montefiore mural project as Monte de Flores, created through Creative Art Works’ Public Art Youth Employment Program for NYC Parks. Documented artists connected to the mural team include Riki Sabel, Jonathan Hyman, and Stephanie Diaz; the project itself was produced with Creative Art Works Youth Apprentices after community surveys and design development with local stakeholders.

Riki Sabel

Creative Art Works documents Riki Sabel as part of the Monte de Flores mural team, linking the park’s art to a named working artist rather than an anonymous backdrop.

Jonathan Hyman

Jonathan Hyman’s Creative Art Works bio explicitly names his work on the Montefiore Square mural, strengthening the claim that the site already carries authored cultural value.

Stephanie Diaz

Stephanie Diaz is also publicly documented as a contributor to Monte de Flores, making the redesign argument about stewardship of existing artistic labor, not replacement of an empty wall.

Design Proposals

Six Moves to
Reclaim the Plaza

Targeted interventions across spatial design, infrastructure, and programming — converting Montefiore from a pass-through into a cultural plaza.

Mural-Facing Seating Alcoves

Curved bench clusters installed opposite each mural panel, angled so the seated sightline faces the art. Planters and low hedges define each alcove as a spatial "room," disrupting the corridor logic.

Vendor Kiosks & Market Zones

Modular kiosk units at the park's widest section create a community market strip. Vendors face inward, drawing foot traffic off the through-path and into the cultural space of the plaza.

Mural Uplighting & Interpretive Panels

Warm 2700K uplighting extends cultural life into evenings. Weatherproof interpretive panels beside each artwork share artist biographies and mural narratives — a self-guided cultural tour.

Improved Pedestrian Crossings

Raised pedestrian tables with contrasting paver surfaces at both park entrances slow vehicle speeds and signal pedestrian priority. Curb extensions increase safety and plaza presence.

Annual Public Art Commissions

An annual open call for Bronx-based artists to contribute floor murals, sculptural elements, and rotating installations. Keeps the park culturally current and generates recurring press.

Weekend Programming Calendar

A 12-month programming calendar — open mics, mural workshops, film nights, and Saturday markets — co-designed with the community board and local schools. Culture creates dwell time.

Weekend Programming

The Cultural Calendar

Year-round programming converts the redesigned space into a living community institution — generating consistent foot traffic and cultural activation alongside physical improvements.

Monthly · Saturday

Bronx Arts Market

12 vendor stalls featuring Bronx-based artists, printmakers, ceramicists, and makers. Kiosks open 10am–5pm. Free entry, all ages.

Vendor
Monthly · Friday Evening

Mural Cinema Night

Outdoor film screenings where the selected film connects thematically to one of the park's murals. Pre-screening artist talk included.

Arts & Film
Bi-Weekly · Sunday

Open Mic & Performance

Community-run open mic with acoustic music, poetry, and spoken word. Held on the new performance platform, facing the mural wall.

Performance
Quarterly · Weekend

Live Mural Workshop

Public mural-making workshops led by commissioned artists and local youth programs. Participants become co-creators of the cultural space.

Workshop

Community-Designed

Events co-produced with Community Board 4, local schools, and the Bronx BID.

Vendor Revenue

Market fees fund park maintenance; vendor slots prioritize Bronx-based small businesses.

Dwell Time Target

Programming + design working together targets 15–30 min average dwell, up from <2 min.

Vendor Revenue

At 12 stalls × $75 per Saturday × 52 weeks, the market generates about $46,800 per year before sponsorships or special-event fees.

Funding Gap

The remaining maintenance gap is approximately $13,200 annually, which could be covered through seasonal grants, business sponsorships, or higher-fee signature events.

Why This Matters

This makes the proposal financially legible: vendor fees do not magically fund culture; they offset a defined maintenance obligation tied to a defined capital investment.

AI Reflection

What AI Helped With
and What It Did Not

AI was used as a drafting and formatting tool during the making of this project. It helped with structuring sections of the website, polishing wording, and revising the code so the presentation matched the proposal more clearly. For this final revision, AI was also used to help add a budget section, an AI reflection, and artist attribution after teacher feedback.

AI did not visit the site, generate the design concept on its own, or make the final judgments about what should be proposed. The site argument, the redesign logic, the selection of features, and the final decisions about what stayed in the project were mine. I reviewed and edited the AI-assisted material, checked sources for the mural attribution, and decided how the project should read as a whole.

In other words, AI helped me draft and revise, but it did not replace the actual design thinking, selection, or responsibility for the final work.
